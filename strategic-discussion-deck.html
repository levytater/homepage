06 · The macro context

Where consulting is going.

The thesis from a recent industry pod, paraphrased:

The pyramid of junior labor producing analysis and decks is the part AI eats first. What survives is the work that combines an intelligence platform, domain expertise, and change management.

The model shifts from experts-to-rent to transformation operating system. The firms that keep selling headcount lose.

The remaining surface area is where the defensible work lives.

The new shape — three axes

  1. Intelligence platform. The agents, models, and tooling that do the actual work.
  2. Domain expertise. The operator-grade knowledge that tells the platform what to do.
  3. Change management. The human capacity to make a client organization adopt and trust the new system.

07 · A temporary advantage

The window is shorter than it looks.

Today, agentic capabilities are difficult to learn, difficult to adapt, and require massive change management to actually implement inside a business. That is the gap I am positioned to serve.

But these tools are only a few months into their lifecycle. Every Frontier Lab release makes them easier to use, easier to deploy, more self-serve.

The strategic question I am wrestling with:

Am I going to dedicate all this time to becoming an expert in how to use and adopt these tools — when that knowledge becomes irrelevant the moment models are smart and flexible enough to drop into any workflow, any tech stack? When the Frontier Labs themselves move into the verticals and productize the roles inside them directly?

09 · How I work — the WAT framework

Workflows. Agents. Tools.

The methodology behind the build, three layers, transparent and named.

W  Workflows

The client's process as it actually runs today, written as a SOP a new hire could read on day one. I do not skip this step. I do not delegate it. The first deliverable in any engagement is the WAT map.

A  Agents

The decision logic. What does "qualified" mean for this portfolio? What is an automatic decline? What needs a human gate? Every rule explicit, every judgment call surfaced.

T  Tools

The deterministic scripts that execute. SMS sender. Doc reviewer. Database updater. Boring on purpose — boring is reliable.

"We use AI" sounds like a hobby. "WAT-mapped operations" sounds like a system. It is one. It also gives the client something they own when I leave — a map, not a dependency.

10 · What I think today

My current hypothesis. Held loosely.

If forced to commit to a shape this week, here is my best guess.

  1. Phase 1 — Build (now → ~12 months). Lead with implementation. Bespoke, premium, low-volume. Free pilot with one design partner; paid build engagements with the next three. The work teaches me which form compounds.
  2. Phase 2 — Pattern (months 12–24). Productize the patterns that repeat. Forms-fill engine, leasing pipeline, doc-review skill — each becomes a packaged capability. Margin improves. Repeat clients become the ICP.
  3. Phase 3 — Platform (months 24+). Either the SaaS thesis is real and platform becomes the business, or the consulting practice has compounded into an "intelligence platform plus domain expertise plus change management" firm — and the SaaS is a feature, not the business.

The risk: Phase 1 is cash-positive enough to anesthetize me into staying there forever. Many implementation shops never graduate.

The build-phase questions I have not yet answered.

  1. Scope. How do I scope the pilot so I am not spending endless hours building for them, for free?
  2. Repurposability. How do I make sure that what I build is shift-able, repurpose-able, and modular enough to be reused in the next engagement — instead of dying as one-off custom code?
  3. Element selection. Do I cherry-pick only the elements of their process where I am confident other clients will have the same problem — and skip the parts that are too specific or too basic to generalize?

  4. What is the moat in eighteen months? When foundation models commoditize the build, what is left? My current bet — trust, domain depth, change management. Is that enough?
  1. The day job. The same trend that makes my consulting business possible is the trend that ends roles like the one I just took. Stay until forced out, or jump on my own timing?
  2. The US move. Build for Ontario only, or build cross-border from day one?
  3. Scaling without losing operator-grade quality. Hire? Subcontract? Train operators inside client orgs? Stay solo?
